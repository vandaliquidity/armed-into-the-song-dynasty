Returning capital specialists exchange training: recruits teach traditional mounted/foot combat and learn the team's weapons and methods. Signaller reconnects with scholar's family and encounters Qin Hui, considering whether changed events could alter his trajectory instead of assuming future guilt. River crossing combines boats, artillery, wind-borne balloons and bridge engineers. Balloon attacks cause friendly fire; landing troops hold against adaptive counterattacks while crossing order changes to prioritise crossbows and guns. Nearly a thousand casualties, damaged balloons and bridge repair follow victory. Medic commands Linhe's mixed garrison and hospital; captain retains bridge defence, builds warning posts and anticipates a rear attack. Xia seeks Jin protection and plans fallback from its capital, then sends thirty thousand against Linhe. Medic holds three fronts while cross-river guns sink the boat attack. Coalition discipline, medical command and rear security remain central.

## Volume IV chapters 79–93 (global 400–414)

Linhe relief becomes a converging battle: the prince charges prematurely, forcing Yue's concealed cavalry to commit; the medic sallies with a mixed garrison, while the captain returns across the river against the northern attackers. Pursuit prevents Xia regrouping. The prince's minor wound and demands for personal care expose the hospital's competing obligations; the medic rejects his entitlement and warns that royal recklessness endangers everyone responsible for him. Capital interludes develop the signaller's relationship with Mo Qingyuan, luxury gifts and family patronage, culminating in an imperial marriage decree. His two trained women operatives enter the princess's household as protective agents against internal threats.

Xiping/Lingzhou falls after concentrated bombardment that consumes almost the entire available explosive stock, damages civilian homes, and intimidates the next capital. This is the novel's speculative military technology, not verified historical capability. The captain negotiates Xia withdrawal from Xingqing rather than pursuing total conquest: supply limits, court politics, strategic breathing room, and Helan iron, coal and especially copper mines matter. Xia withdraws west with its army and court intact; Deshun occupies the city without destruction. Other Song armies exploit the collapsing frontier, while Jin occupies another Xia district and threatens intervention. The court observer receives exaggerated credit and treasure, then secretly plans to detach talented officers from the captain. Victory creates political vulnerability as well as territory.


## Volume IV chapters 94–111 (global 415–432)

The prince and observer scheme to bring the medic to the capital through a new hospital, despite her refusal; her companions' protection also becomes paternalistic confinement. The princess dismisses then accepts her protective operatives amid jealousy, gains divorce with her child retained, and fails to overturn the signaller's imperial marriage. The emperor sends him west for a six-month wedding leave. He resists an impulsive assassination of the visiting Jin envoy, recognising that killing one man cannot replace strategic preparation.

The captain becomes Xiping duke, Qinghe military governor and western commissioner, formally commanding Qinghe and Deshun plus other troops in the occupied region. The sniper takes Qinghe command, Feng transfers to Deshun, and Tie Shan/Black Tiger inherit the Longcheng formation. Yue and Yao Pingzhong are transferred away as court counterweights, creating real command vacancies; the captain discovers Wu Jie and Wu Lin in the subordinate ranks. Trusted county officials receive provisional frontier civil appointments; the old village head temporarily runs the home county. These promotions mix competence, old obligations and patronage. The new rank also enables official military-store procurement: money alone had not solved shortages of sulphur and saltpetre. Helan mines and regional purchasing underpin a six-month rearmament target.

Jin obtains crossbow plans but struggles with workmanship. Its leaders choose proxy warfare under Xia flags and political attempts to discredit or recruit the captain, avoiding open war until prepared. A roughly 40,000-strong proxy threat advances on Youzhou's small garrison. In the capital, the princess takes over the commercial concern, coercively cancels distributor exclusivity, raises wholesale prices and compels purchases. Palace orders surge; cosmetics require increased western shipments while alcohol has spare capacity. An official challenges royal interference in commerce. Her success rests partly on privilege and threats, not simply superior management.

## Volume IV chapters 112–129 (global 433–450)

The signaller brings commercial earnings west for military purchasing, but regional authority does not compel other circuits' stores: central approval still permits local retention and negotiation. Royal intervention secures half the Luoyang stock; he and the prince assault the official and deny it using mutual testimony. Heavy carts then require over a month to reach the frontier, separating money, permission, purchase and actual delivery. The princess faces commercial opposition and the emperor refuses the prince's proposed broken betrothal; the observer also sees the medic as a potential hostage to control the captain.

The Youzhou relief force falls into a Xia frontal/Jin rear trap. Of 2,000 men only 171 remain standing; Black Tiger is badly wounded, Tie Shan loses an arm but survives. Friendly fire, panic, depleted grenades and superior enemy close combat defeat equipment advantage. Main-force relief saves the survivors; the medic organises a wider hospital response and rechecks the presumed dead. Medical claims in this fiction are not clinical guidance. Han Shizhong and 3,700 existing garrison veterans transfer permanently into Qinghe, allowing its restricted equipment, pay and training system to apply. Wu Jie and Wu Lin gain a mounted crossbow command.

Tongwan's abandoned tribal garrison negotiates disarmament while retaining homes, fields and graves. The medic buys buildings for a hospital serving future civilians as well as troops. The captain uses himself and his train as bait in the Hongliu valley, while Wu's cavalry carries extra water and remounts across the desert to attack the exit. A trained balloon unit joins the flanking force. Jin detects scouts but misjudges their scale; its rear guard and counterattack inflict further Song losses. Balloon attacks have losses to archery, and the sniper needs three shots to kill Lou Shi. His son withdraws with survivors and the body. Mitong's layered forts surrender after the proxy coalition collapses; Li Liangfu dies and his wife kills herself and their daughters. The eastern occupied region is secured, at substantial human cost.


## Volume IV chapters 130–144 (global 451–465)

At Jingzhao the signaller bargains to clear a bandit stronghold in exchange for scarce military materials, also securing transfers of Qu Duan and staff specialist Huang You. Mixed teams use signals and coordinated pursuit; he follows retreating attackers to their base. Fang Baihua is captured and executed for massacres, while imprisoned Fang Qifo is recruited for the coming Jin war despite his rebel status. These are fictionalised encounters, not reliable biographies. The court grants the captain one year of exceptional appointment authority, after which normal central control is promised. The Luoyang official is removed after royal allies revive an old case, revealing commercial retaliation through public institutions.

Wu Jie receives the expanded 5,000-man Beiwei formation at Mitong; Han receives 4,000 Qinghe troops at Tongwan, forming mutually supporting frontier positions. The captain argues that this western threat to Datong may prevent the historical two-column Jingkang campaign, but still anticipates Jin war. Helan extraction and minting support expansion; the engineer proposes fewer artillery types and a later maritime future. These are intentions, not completed outcomes. Jin now fields copied crossbows, seeks other technologies via Song procurement channels and favours internal political sabotage over immediate attack. Lou Shi's funeral includes the court's intended human sacrifice; his widow dies, and the decree is withdrawn. The narration idealises her death; the ledger records it as tragedy rather than an admirable gameplay reward.

The Jin envoy arrives after the captain's victory, reassesses his leverage, and unsuccessfully seeks influence through gifts to the medic, mistakenly believing her his wife. She studies the brief and leads talks despite sexist objections. She turns territorial demands toward Jin responsibility for the proxy attacks, threatens a claim on Yunnei and demands compensation. The envoy postpones agreement pending his government's decision and asks to meet the captain. No indemnity or territorial transfer has yet been secured.

## Volume IV chapters 145–165 (global 466–486)

A staged military demonstration exaggerates explosive power and the captain issues a thirty-day Yunnei ultimatum. Jin instead sends 50,000 gold directly to the Song court, preserves Yunnei, renews the alliance and threatens war against further incursion. Its letter deliberately praises the medic's negotiations to encourage her removal from the frontier. The emperor orders her to the capital to lead a crown-prince-funded civilian hospital with nominal autonomy, a house, gold and an honorary title. Court patronage, gender restrictions, the prince's pursuit and Jin strategy converge in the same transfer. The princess is ordered to leave public commerce; she turns toward succession intrigue, encouraging her brother to work against the crown prince while using another faction's support for a different royal candidate.

Homecoming chapters show flourishing Longcheng and Qinghe through shops, workers' better clothing, housing grants for defenders, veterans' livelihoods, a workers' hospital, warehouses, training grounds, animal pens and a gridded street plan. The community remembers casualties through a cemetery and the captain's wife's new officially funded shrine. The signaller's commercial proceeds return as redeemable paper money, gold and physical materials; the five discuss preserving capital for a future departure from Song. The engineer's six-month production figures are targets contingent on materials. Only 110 special magazine crossbows can be made from salvaged modern vehicle springs; they are reserved for the signaller's small capital contingency force, not general mass issue. He also requests balloons for emergency evacuation. Qu and Huang join the western apparatus; Wu Song, Yang Zhi and Fang Qifo stay personally attached to the signaller.

The signaller marries Mo Qingyuan at Longcheng, with his companions and allied officials attending. She is explicitly fifteen, and the narrative says the marriage is not consummated. Record the household and political connection without adapting sexualised descriptions. His friends recognise the emperor may prevent his return to the capital. A local official drops the earlier household-murder investigation after seeing the protagonists' power, another case of impunity. Mo's brother receives a proposed Yanzhou appointment. These peaceful celebrations and memorial scenes make the threatened home region concrete rather than merely an income screen.
